The last hand-made page

In a back office of a 112-year-old Silesian weekly, a subeditor still cuts headlines from a laser printer with scissors and fixes them to the page with wax. She is not nostalgic. She simply believes a headline is a physical thing.

In the back room of a 112-year-old weekly in Silesia, Halina Bednarek cuts a headline out of a sheet of laser-printed paper with a pair of scissors, trims the left margin with a knife, and fixes the strip of paper to a physical page with a block of warm wax. The page lies flat on a drafting table, part of a sixty-four-page issue that has been assembled by hand, in order, every Wednesday for the last thirty-one years.

Asked whether she has considered layout software, Halina — who is sixty-four and has the calm of a woman who has never missed a deadline — looks at the question the way one might look at a proposal to reorganise the kitchen. “The computer,” she says, “makes headlines that look like they were typed by a computer.”

Sixty-four pages by hand

The workflow has not changed since the 1990s: journalists write in a plain text editor, a layout team sets the pages in an old program that runs on a single, carefully labelled computer, and the pages are printed at full size on a laser printer, one per sheet. Every headline is then cut out, kerned by hand where the spacing is wrong, and pressed onto the page with wax — because wax, unlike glue, lets you lift a headline and move it six millimetres to the left without ruining everything.

A full issue is sixty-four pages. The design desk predicts, at the start of each week, how many cut-and-paste headlines will be needed; the record, set during a municipal elections special in 2021, is 214. The subeditors keep a tally on a whiteboard, in pen, next to the kettle.

The machine in the corner

In the corner of the room stands the paper’s failed pagination system — a desktop computer bought in 2010, connected to a monitor that still displays the setup screen of the layout suite the editors tried for six months. Everyone in the building calls it “the automation”. It has not been turned on since 2011. Its power cable is wrapped around its base in a neat loop, which is the closest thing the newsroom has to a eulogy.

The decision to go back to paper-and-wax was not romantic; it was economic. The layout suite produced pages that required a designer with a diploma, a licence fee that grew every year and, crucially, it made the pages look like every other page in Poland. The hand-made pages took longer, but they cost nothing, needed no licence, and — the editors claim — the readers could tell the difference. Circulation in the district has declined, they admit, but slower than the regional average.

A headline is a physical thing. You should be able to pick it up, turn it over, and put it back where it belongs. Halina Bednarek, chief subeditor

What automation saves (and takes)

The standard argument for moving to modern tools is time. The hand-made page takes forty-five minutes; the automated page takes four. What the automation debate rarely puts on the table is where that saved time goes. Here, it mostly goes nowhere: the weekly has a fixed number of staff, a fixed number of advertisers, and a fixed number of pages to fill. The layout software did not make the paper better; it made the paper faster at being the same.

The editors reached a working compromise that would horrify management consultants. Journalists type their copy on phones and laptops. The copy is set and paginated on the machine. Then it is printed, cut, and re-assembled by hand — not to change the text, but to change the rhythm: to give a headline breathing room, to pull a paragraph back from the edge, to make a page feel like it was made for a reader rather than for a template.

Halina admits the system is absurd. She says it in the tone of someone describing a beloved, inefficient house. “It is absurd,” she agrees, “and it works.”

The economics of refusal

The paper’s finances argue, quietly, that the absurdity is subsidised by smallness. A weekly with a circulation of a few thousand can afford to underinvest in technology because its readers are not choosing between it and a website; they are choosing between it and nothing. The paper’s paywall — a small, stubborn implementation that charges readers for single archival articles — brings in enough to pay for the laser printer and the wax, and the editors treat precisely that as the point: the revenue exists to keep the craft, not to replace it.

It would be easy to present the wax table as a monument to nostalgia, and the editors would be the first to object. The craft, they argue, is not the method; the craft is the judgement. Cutting a headline by hand forces you to look at the headline, to feel its weight, to notice that “The Council Votes” and “The Council Decides” are not interchangeable. Automation did not remove that judgement. It just got very good at hiding it.

Wax and deadlines

On Wednesday afternoon the issue is closed the way it has been closed for decades: the last page is printed, cut, and pressed; the subeditors sign their initials on the top sheet in pencil; and the pages are carried to the back-shop in a stack, where they will be scanned, paginated, and sent to the printer — a process that, in a final irony, is entirely digital.

Halina cleans her scissors, locks the wax block in the drawer, and writes the next week’s headline tally at the top of a fresh whiteboard. Sixty-four pages. Somewhere behind her, the automation continues to gather dust in its corner, its cable wrapped neatly, waiting for a decision that is not coming.

“Some things,” Halina says, “are made better by hand. A headline is one of them. A decision is another.” She says this without looking up, which is how she says everything.
