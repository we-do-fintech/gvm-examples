The town that read the fine print

When a local paper moved a century of archives behind a paywall, a retired teacher kept a carbon-copy diary of every story that vanished. For nineteen months, she was the town’s memory — and nobody asked her to be.

By Jan Nowicki · 19 Feb 2026 · 7 min read

The first thing Barbara Wiśniewska noticed was the obituaries. For seventy years she had read the weekly Nadodrze from the front page to the classifieds, and on the second Thursday of March the obituaries were simply no longer there. Not gone in the sense of being delayed — gone in the sense of having been removed, twelve years back, from the paper’s online archive, along with nineteen other issues from a single summer.

Barbara is retired, seventy-nine years old, and patient in the way of people who have spent forty-one years in front of a blackboard. She did not complain to the editor. She bought a ledger, the kind with numbered pages and a cloth spine, and started writing down everything the paper removed: the issue, the page, the headline, and a one-line summary of what the story had been.

Eight cardboard boxes

The paper had gone digital the way most local papers did: the typographer scanned the bound annuals of the 1950s, the archive vendor OCR’d everything with a cheerful optimism about optical character recognition, and somebody — nobody remembers who — decided that the first twelve years of the website would live behind a paywall, one archive article at a time.

What the editorial team did not anticipate was how the town treated that archive. Nadodrze is a district of 27,000 people, and its century-old weekly is the only place where a family’s public history exists in one continuous run: births, marriages, deaths, the licensing of a butcher’s shop, the opening of a bridge, the names of the children who won the spelling contest in 1987. When the archive moved behind a paywall, the town noticed mostly because Barbara told it to.

She started with the obituaries because they were the hardest loss. A death notice is not really a document of death; it is a document of a life, compressed, and for many families it is the only printed record that survives. When the paywall took those, it took the town’s memory at its most tender point.

The paywall, politely

Barbara’s method was meticulous and conspicuously fair. She never copied the articles. She summarised them in her own words, keyed to issue and page, and she checked her summaries against the free previews that the site still served. When the editors, at first confused and then amused, asked her about the ledger, she explained that she was not against payment — she paid, in fact, for a subscription. She simply believed a town archive should have an index, and the paper’s index now stopped at the paywall.

She was not, she insists, working against the paper. Her subscription pays for the issue every week, and she reads the paywalled archive herself when the library computer lets her; the ledger is referenced the same way the paper references its own issues, down to the page numbers. In her own eyes she is doing the job the site’s search box used to do, before the paywall taught it to forget.

An archive behind a paywall is still an archive. But a town that cannot find its own past is something else. Barbara Wiśniewska, retired teacher

The editor of Nadodrze, a man in his fifties who had once written a column about freedom of information, later told me this was the most polite political argument in the town’s recent history. Barbara did not write letters to the council. She did not organise a petition. She wrote down, issue by issue, everything that was disappearing, and she shared her summaries — on paper, of course — with the local library.

What the archive knows about us

What Barbara’s ledger revealed, once it grew to eight hundred entries, was that the paywall’s removals were not random. The paper’s online archive had been assembled in three batches, by three different vendors, and the oldest batch had the worst OCR. Articles from the 1960s and 1970s — including an entire summer of the town’s history — had been judged “unreadable” and pulled from the search index rather than fixed.

This is the quiet tragedy of digital archives: deletion is rarely malicious and almost always administrative. A licensing agreement lapses, a database migration drops a field, an OCR confidence score falls below a threshold, and a decade of local history stops existing from the reader’s point of view. Nobody deleted the data; it simply became uneconomical to keep it findable.

The town council, embarrassed into action, voted a small grant to restore the summer of 1988. Barbara attended the vote, sat in the second row, and said nothing. The ledger had done its work.

A civic ledger

The wider lesson is not that paywalls are bad. Nadodrze’s paywall is, by local standards, a success: it keeps the weekly solvent, and its archives now fund the paper’s digital editor. The lesson is about adjacency — the fact that an archive is not a product but a public good, and that a paywall drawn across it draws a line through the town itself.

Barbara finished the first ledger in November, nineteen months after she started. The editors of Nadodrze, having been gently shamed and then genuinely moved, did two things: they restored the orphaned summer of 1988, and they printed the first page of her ledger on the letters page, exactly as it looked, with the note: “Indexing the town, one issue at a time.”

She has started a second ledger. There is talk that the library will digitise it, which she views with the deep suspicion of someone who has watched three OCR vendors fail. She will probably be right about that too.

Before we left, Barbara showed me the drawer where she keeps the receipts — every subscription confirmation, neatly dated. “The paper needs its money,” she said, closing the drawer. “It also needs to remember what the money is for.” The editor, who had come to say goodbye, said nothing at all.
